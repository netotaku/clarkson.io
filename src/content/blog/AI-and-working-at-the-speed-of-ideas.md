I've been building websites and software for more than 25 years. For most of that time, the process has been broadly the same. Somebody has a problem, we work out how to solve it, estimate the effort, establish a budget and build something that hopefully meets their needs.

If we're building a product, the calculation becomes more complicated. How many people have this problem? Will they pay to solve it? Can we build something reusable? Is there enough of a market to justify the investment?

Software has traditionally been expensive to make. That expense has shaped what gets built.

But something fundamental is changing.

## The 3D printer moment for software

I think AI-assisted development has reached something approaching the software equivalent of 3D printing.

Before affordable 3D printing, if you needed a peculiar little plastic component, you either found something close enough, adapted an existing product or went without. Manufacturing something for an audience of one rarely made economic sense.

Now you can design exactly what you need and print it.

AI is beginning to do the same thing for software.

I've seen music producers building their own VST plugins rather than searching for an existing one that does almost what they want. I've seen people on Reddit creating dedicated desktop applications for oddly specific personal requirements.

They're not launching startups. They're not looking for customers. They're making tools for themselves.

And why wouldn't they?

If you can build exactly what you need, why spend hours searching for a product that gets you 80% of the way there, then pay a monthly subscription for the privilege?

## What happens to SaaS?

Software as a Service is built around a fairly straightforward proposition. Lots of people have similar problems, so we build a common solution and charge them to use it.

It's a perfectly sensible model when building software is expensive.

But what happens when the cost of creating a bespoke solution becomes negligible compared with the cost of buying one?

I'm already noticing the change in my own workflow.

I haven't visited Stack Overflow for months. It wasn't a conscious decision. I simply stopped needing to go there.

I still use Google, but considerably less than I used to.

I no longer feel the same need for specialist HTML email development tools such as Litmus or Email on Acid. AI can inspect the markup, identify likely compatibility issues and help me fix them. That doesn't mean it can reproduce the rendering behaviour of every email client, but it's changed what I need from those services.

Email housekeeping is another example. Why learn another application's interface when I can describe what I want done?

These aren't all examples of software being replaced by newly written software. Some are services being absorbed into a more general-purpose tool.

But the direction is similar.

The value of an application is increasingly being questioned when the same result can be achieved without it.

Of course, SaaS isn't going to disappear overnight. Businesses still need reliable infrastructure, shared data, security, compliance and systems that somebody is responsible for maintaining.

But I suspect the market for small, single-purpose subscription products is going to become much harder to defend.

And that's before we consider what happens when people start making their own alternatives.

## Working at the speed of ideas

For me, the most exciting change isn't the money saved on subscriptions.

It's the freedom to build exactly what I want.

I've always had more ideas than time to build them. Games, little utilities, applications that solve problems in my own particular way.

Historically, even with decades of development experience, starting something meant making a commitment.

You'd need to set up the project, choose the technology, build the foundations and work through the inevitable problems. And if you were going to invest that much time, you naturally started asking whether the idea was worth pursuing.

Would anybody else use it? Could it become something bigger? Was there an existing product that did roughly the same thing?

Vibe coding has changed that calculation.

I'm taking on more ambitious projects because I no longer need to justify the effort against a potential audience.

If I want a tool that works in a particular way, I can make one.

I don't need to accommodate anybody else's requirements. I don't need to design for every possible use case. I don't need to build a configuration screen for options I'll never change.

And I don't need to compromise because the software I'm using was designed to satisfy thousands of other people.

**We're working at the speed of ideas, building software for an audience of one.**

This isn't simply about making prototypes or experimenting to see whether something might work.

It's about making software that actually works for me.

A small application can be complete, useful and successful without ever becoming a commercial product.

It doesn't need to scale to thousands of users. It doesn't need a marketing strategy, a subscription model or a development roadmap.

It just needs to do what I want it to do.

## Nobody asked for this

There's something liberating about not having to justify every idea.

For years, my professional work has been driven by other people's requirements. Understand the user, establish the problem, design the solution, deliver against expectations.

And rightly so. That's what clients pay for.

But when I'm making something for myself, those rules don't necessarily apply.

I don't need a discovery workshop. I don't need market research. I don't need to demonstrate demand or identify a route to monetisation.

I can make something because I want it to exist.

As Alfred says of the Joker in *The Dark Knight*, some men just want to watch the world burn.

Some of us just want to build another app nobody asked for.

There's a similar freedom in the way I approach making music these days.

I make music because I enjoy making it. I like the process, the experimentation and the satisfaction of hearing an idea turn into something tangible.

If somebody else likes it, that's brilliant. But it's no longer the primary motivation.

I'm beginning to feel the same way about software.

I don't need to make something other people want. I can make something I want.

And unlike a commercial product, I don't have to keep expanding it to satisfy an audience.

When it works for me, it's finished.

## The return of the personal computer

There's an interesting historical parallel here.

In the early days of personal computing, people wrote programs because they could. They made games, utilities, demos and strange experiments, often sharing them with other enthusiasts.

Much of it had no commercial purpose.

As the web matured, software became increasingly professionalised. Development became a business activity. Products needed users, users needed journeys, journeys needed conversions.

Even side projects began to feel like potential startups.

Somewhere along the way, we started treating the ability to build something as an obligation to turn it into a business.

AI-assisted development might reverse some of that.

Not by removing the need for professional software development, but by making bespoke software accessible to people who would never previously have considered building their own tools.

A musician can make a plugin. A designer can make an application. Somebody with an oddly specific problem can create a solution that works for exactly one person.

There's no need to find a market.

And when the cost of making something is low enough, there's no particular reason to settle for somebody else's idea of how it should work.

## The death of SaaS might be the wrong story

I don't actually think SaaS is going to die.

There will always be value in software that's dependable, supported, secure and shared between people. And creating your own tools introduces responsibilities that a subscription normally takes care of.

But I do think the assumption that software needs to be packaged, marketed and sold is being challenged.

We've spent decades building products for the largest possible audience, because that's what the economics demanded.

Now we're entering a period where software can be made for an audience of one.

And that changes more than the business model.

It changes our relationship with the things we make.

**The most interesting consequence of AI might not be that we need less software. It might be that we no longer need someone else's permission, priorities or business model to have the software we want.**
